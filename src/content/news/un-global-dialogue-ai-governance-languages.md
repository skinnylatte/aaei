The first UN Global Dialogue on Artificial Intelligence Governance met in Geneva on 6 and 7 July 2026, opening the week of the AI for Good Global Summit. It brought together more than 3,000 participants, including delegations from 163 countries. At its centre was the first report of the UN's new [Independent International Scientific Panel on AI](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/sites/default/files/2026-07/en_executive_summary.pdf): 40 experts, co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa.

Language came up again and again. These are the parts that matter most for anyone testing AI outside English.

## "That's not a footnote"

Presenting the panel's work, Bengio set out the categories of risk. Ressa followed with the people: "three findings, small ones, because the big picture can sometimes hide the people inside it." The first was about Tigrinya.

> In Tigrinya, spoken by 7 to 9 million people in Eritrea and northern Ethiopia, machine translation turned smallpox into syphilis, gonorrhea into diabetes…

It also turned "you have been given intravenous antibiotics" into "you have been given intravenous insecticides". "That's not a footnote," she said. "That can be life-threatening."

## Only a handful of 7,000 languages

Anna Korhonen, who leads the panel's working group on cultural and individual flourishing, made linguistic inclusion the first of its four priority areas, those where the evidence is now strong enough for policymaking:

> We have over 7,000 human languages in the world, but current AI reflects only a handful of them, mostly the majority languages of the Global North. As a result, most of humanity is currently unable to access or benefit from AI using their native languages.

It is not inevitable, she added: at least 1,000 more languages already have the foundations needed for AI. Getting there will take systemic changes in how AI is built, and targeted investment in capacity.

The panel's report summary makes two related points. AI still struggles to perform strongly "across human languages and cultures". And the expertise to evaluate it is concentrated where AI is built: compute, evaluation expertise and data, including data for different languages, sit in a few places, "leaving most Member States dependent on systems they cannot build, inspect, audit or fully adapt to local context." It also notes the upside: front-line health workers in low-resource settings already use AI tools adapted to local languages to deliver better care.

## What the delegations said

The [co-chairs' summary](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/sites/default/files/2026-09/ai_dialogue_co-chairs_summary.pdf) records the same concerns from governments and civil society:

- **Mistranslation does real harm.** Participants warned that AI trained mainly on a handful of majority languages excludes most of the world's linguistic and cultural diversity, and "cited documented harms from mistranslation".
- **Inclusion should be a design requirement.** They proposed treating linguistic and cultural inclusion "as a design requirement rather than an afterthought", building on national and grassroots work on smaller, locally adapted models.
- **Evaluations need local context.** Low-resource and Indigenous languages were emphasised, "with attention to local knowledge and contexts in datasets, models and evaluations".
- **Measure what matters.** One proposal was to judge AI's success by different metrics, including "languages thriving in the digital world, and communities whose knowledge is reflected."

## Why it matters

The Tigrinya example is exactly the kind of failure that never shows up in an English benchmark, and exactly the kind that hurts someone. Finding it takes people who speak the language testing the system on the things they actually need, like medical instructions.

The Dialogue set out the problem. The AI for Good Summit that followed went further into how to fix it: ITU's AI Standards Report 2026 calls for evaluation co-created with local communities and for regional testing capacity.
